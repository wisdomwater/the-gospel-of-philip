# Day 22 — Receive Resurrection Now

> “Those who say they'll die first and then rise are mistaken. If they don't receive the resurrection while they're living, they won't receive anything when they die.”
>
> — *The Gospel of Philip*

We are accustomed to placing resurrection at the end of the story. Life happens first, death follows, and somewhere beyond the boundary we cannot see, resurrection waits. It becomes the great promise of later. But what if resurrection also belongs to now?

The words are deliberately unsettling. They pull resurrection out of the distant future and place it directly into the life we are already living. Whatever resurrection ultimately means beyond death, we are being asked to discover its life before we die.

This is not the first time we have encountered the reversal. The Lord arose first and then died, turning our familiar sequence upside down. Resurrection is not merely what happens after death; it is the life that makes it possible to pass through death without being defined by it.

Perhaps we have already experienced fragments of this. There are seasons when some part of us seems to disappear. A relationship ends, a dream collapses, a role that gave us identity is taken away, or a version of ourselves we depended upon can no longer survive. We remain physically alive, but something has died.

Then, slowly, life appears where we did not expect it. We laugh again without forcing it. We become curious about something new, or discover that the future no longer looks completely empty. Nothing has undone the loss, yet something within us has begun to rise.

Resurrection does not erase the grave. The risen Christ still carries wounds. Whatever resurrection means, it does not require pretending that suffering never happened. The wound can remain part of the story without remaining the end of the story.

Perhaps that is one way resurrection becomes possible now. We stop waiting for the past to become different before allowing ourselves to live. We stop requiring every wound to disappear before believing we can become whole. What has happened remains true, but it no longer possesses the authority to determine everything that comes next.

Life begins growing around the wound. This can happen in very ordinary ways. Someone who has lived for years beneath shame begins speaking to themselves with kindness. Someone ruled by resentment discovers they can remember the injury without rehearsing revenge.

A stone moves slightly. The movement may be almost invisible. Resurrection does not always arrive with angels, earthquakes, and an empty tomb. Sometimes it appears as the willingness to get out of bed, make breakfast, answer the phone, take a walk, or try once more.

Small acts can carry enormous life. Perhaps this is why resurrection must be received. We cannot force ourselves into new life any more than a seed can command itself to grow. Something is given, and our part is to become available to it.

Faith receives. We may receive only enough life for this moment. Tomorrow may still be uncertain, and the old fear may return before the day is over. Yet resurrection does not require possessing an entire future; it asks whether we can receive the life being offered now.

One breath can be enough. This changes how we think about spiritual transformation. We may imagine that becoming whole means constructing a better version of ourselves through enough discipline, knowledge, and effort. Resurrection offers a stranger possibility: perhaps the deepest life is not something we build but something we awaken to.

The life is already moving beneath the soil. There are places within us that appear barren because we cannot yet see what is happening underground. Silence feels empty, grief feels final, and waiting feels like nothing is happening. Yet seeds do much of their work in darkness.

The hidden life does not need our constant observation. This is difficult for those of us who want evidence. We want to know that prayer is working, healing is progressing, and our effort is producing measurable change. When we cannot see movement, we assume there is none.

But resurrection is comfortable with hiddenness. A body lies in a tomb before anyone sees it alive again. A seed breaks open beneath the ground before a green shoot appears. Much of transformation happens beyond the reach of measurement.

Perhaps we can learn to trust what is growing unseen. Receiving resurrection now also changes the way we approach death. We do not need to pretend that death is easy or that the unknown beyond it carries no fear. The body still knows how fiercely it wants to live.

But perhaps death becomes less foreign when we have practiced resurrection. We have already released identities that could not come with us. We have already discovered life on the other side of losses we once believed would destroy us. Again and again, we have learned that an ending can be real without being ultimate.

We have practiced rising. Perhaps this is the invitation hidden within every transformation we have encountered. Water washes away what no longer needs to cling to us, fire changes what cannot remain as it was, and light reveals a life deeper than the identities we constructed in darkness. The bridal chamber gathers us into union so that we can emerge wearing something that death cannot easily name.

Resurrection has been moving through all of it. So do not wait for the grave to begin eternal life. Receive whatever portion of resurrection is being offered today. Let something frightened become trusting, something hardened become tender, or something long buried finally reach toward the light.

The future remains mystery. But life is already here. Receive it while you are living.

## Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** Resurrection is here.

> **Exhale:** Let me receive Your life.
