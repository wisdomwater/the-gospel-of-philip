# Day 12 — Seeing in the Light

> “A sighted person and a blind person, when they're both in the dark, aren't different from each other. When the light comes, then the sighted person will see the light, and the blind person will remain in darkness.”
>
> — *The Gospel of Philip*

In complete darkness, sight seems almost useless. Open your eyes or close them and the room looks the same. The furniture is still there, the doorway has not disappeared, and perhaps a window is only a few steps away, but none of these things can be seen.

Then someone lights a lamp.

Nothing in the room has been created by the light. The table was already there, the walls were already standing, and the doorway was already open. Light simply reveals what darkness had made impossible to see.

Perhaps awakening often happens this way.

We imagine that transformation means becoming someone entirely different, as though the person we are must be dismantled and replaced with someone more spiritual. We look at ourselves and see everything unfinished, wounded, inconsistent, and afraid. Then we imagine that somewhere far ahead waits the person God might finally be pleased to love.

But what if some of what we seek is already here?

The pearl did not become valuable when the mud was washed away. Its value had been hidden, not created. The cloth did not invent the color it received from the dyer, but once immersed, it revealed something that could not have been seen before.

Light reveals.

There may be capacities within us that have spent years in darkness. A person who learned early that vulnerability was dangerous may carry great tenderness without knowing how to express it. Someone who has lived beneath shame may possess a deep capacity for compassion, yet remain unable to extend that compassion toward themselves.

The darkness does not prove the room is empty.

Sometimes all that is needed is enough light to begin seeing what has been there. A safe relationship can illuminate something. So can silence, grief, beauty, therapy, friendship, prayer, or the experience of being loved without first becoming impressive.

Suddenly we notice a doorway.

Perhaps we have always been more capable of love than we believed. Perhaps courage was present beneath the fear, waiting for a moment when it could stand. Perhaps the longing we once treated as weakness was actually pointing toward something alive.

Light does not shame the darkness.

It simply enters.

This matters because we often try to change ourselves through accusation. We identify what is wrong and shine a harsh spotlight upon it, believing that enough criticism will force us into transformation. Yet there is a difference between illumination and interrogation.

Divine light does not need to humiliate in order to reveal.

Consider the way morning enters a room. It does not shout at the darkness or demand that it explain itself. Light appears, and darkness gives way simply because something more luminous has arrived.

Perhaps truth works this way too.

We can spend years fighting a pattern without understanding what keeps it alive. Then one day we see the fear beneath it, the wound beneath the fear, or the hunger beneath the behavior. Nothing has yet been fixed, but seeing changes our relationship with what we once experienced only as an enemy.

What was hidden has entered the light.

This kind of seeing requires patience. Our eyes sometimes need time to adjust when we move from darkness into brightness, and there may be truths about ourselves we can only receive gradually. Too much light all at once can make us turn away.

Love gives the eyes time to open.

The same may be true of how we see other people. We often encounter only the visible surface: the irritating behavior, the strange opinion, the defensive reaction, the mistake. Without enough light, we may assume that what we see on the surface is the whole person.

But there is a human inside the human.

Perhaps compassion is a form of illumination. It allows us to see that anger may contain fear, arrogance may conceal insecurity, and withdrawal may have begun as protection. Understanding does not make harmful behavior harmless, but it keeps us from confusing someone's darkness with the whole of who they are.

The light reveals more.

And what is true of others may also be true of God. We carry images formed in darkness: fears, assumptions, inherited names, and stories about what God must be like. Then the face of Christ becomes visible, and another possibility enters the room.

Mercy is illuminated.

Compassion is illuminated.

The Father begins to look like the One whose goodness has always been shining through the Son. Perhaps God has not changed at all. Perhaps something in us has simply begun to see.

There may still be darkness ahead. There may be rooms within us we have not entered and things we are not yet ready to recognize. Awakening does not mean seeing everything at once. It means learning to trust the light.

The doorway was already there. The pearl was already precious. The capacity to see was already waiting.

Let the light come.

## Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** Let Your light enter.

> **Exhale:** Teach me to see.
