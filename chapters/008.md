## Day 8 — Becoming What You See

> “It's impossible for anyone to see anything that really exists unless they become like them. It’s not like the person in the world who sees the sun without becoming a sun, and who sees heaven and earth and everything else without becoming them. That’s the way it is. But you’ve seen something of that place, and have become them. You saw the Spirit, you became spirit; you saw Christ, you became Christ.”
>
> — *The Gospel of Philip*

We usually imagine seeing as something that happens at a distance. There is the thing being observed, and there is the person looking at it. We can admire a mountain without becoming stone, watch the ocean without becoming water, and look into the night sky without becoming a star. The eye receives the image while the observer remains unchanged.

But imagine another kind of seeing. There are things that cannot truly be seen without changing the one who sees them. Spend enough time with someone who is deeply compassionate, and you may begin noticing suffering you once overlooked. Encounter forgiveness when you know you do not deserve it, and mercy may become more than an idea. Some realities reveal themselves by drawing us into them.

“You saw the Spirit, you became spirit; you saw Christ, you became Christ.”

These are startling words. They push beyond the comfortable idea that the spiritual life consists primarily of believing the right things about Christ or even trying to imitate him. The distance between the one who sees and the one who is seen begins to disappear, until seeing becomes participation and knowledge becomes transformation.

Perhaps we already understand this more than we realize. We become like what we give our attention to. Spend years looking for reasons to be angry, and the world will provide an endless supply. Train your eyes to notice threat, comparison, failure, and insult, and eventually these things begin shaping the interior world from which you see everything else. What we behold does not always remain outside us.

The opposite is also true. Attend to beauty and you begin noticing beauty in places you once passed without seeing. Practice gratitude and ordinary things become capable of surprising you again. Sit quietly with another person's suffering without trying to fix it, and compassion begins forming capacities within you that no definition of compassion could have created.

Attention is never entirely passive. This makes contemplation something more than thinking about God. To contemplate Christ is to remain before him long enough that what we see begins working upon us. We look at mercy until mercy becomes imaginable, at compassion until compassion becomes instinctive, at self-giving love until our tightly defended boundaries begin to soften.

We behold, and slowly we become. Perhaps this is why images of God matter so deeply. If the God we continually behold is angry, suspicious, easily offended, and eager to punish, fear may begin taking that shape within us. We may become more watchful of failure, quicker to condemn, and more certain that love must be earned. Without realizing it, we can become like the God we think we see.

But look again at Christ. Watch him touch the person everyone else considers unclean. Watch him eat with those respectable religion has pushed to the margins, notice the overlooked, forgive his enemies, and refuse to let another person's worst moment become the final definition of who they are. Listen as he speaks of lilies and birds, lost sheep and lost coins, seeds disappearing into soil, and a father running down the road toward a child who has finally come home.

What do you see? Perhaps the answer matters because this is not merely information about God. If Christ bears the hidden name of the Father, then what becomes visible in Christ reveals the character of the One who sent him. Mercy does not begin with the Son and reluctantly persuade the Father; the mercy visible in the Son has come from the Father's own heart.

To see Christ, then, is to allow our image of God to be transformed. And to see Christ truly is eventually to become Christlike. Not because our individual selves disappear or because we become Jesus of Nazareth, but because the same quality of life begins taking shape within us. Compassion becomes our compassion. Mercy becomes our mercy. The love we once admired from a distance begins finding hands and feet through our own bodies.

This is the strange movement from seeing to becoming. It also changes the way we approach prayer. Perhaps we do not always need to arrive with words, requests, explanations, and solutions. Sometimes prayer can simply be the practice of placing our attention before the One we desire to resemble.

We look. We remain. We allow ourselves to be seen as we are, while learning to see the One who is before us. Little by little, the separation between prayer and life begins to thin, because what we encounter in silence follows us when we stand up and return to the world.

Then the person who irritates us becomes the place where compassion asks for a body. The wound we carry becomes the place where forgiveness waits to take flesh. The frightened part of ourselves becomes the place where the tenderness we have seen in God asks to be received.

What we behold begins appearing within us. Perhaps this is the deeper purpose of seeing. We are not standing outside the divine life collecting information about it. We are being invited closer and closer until the qualities we once attributed only to God begin becoming visible in human flesh.

You saw the Spirit, and became spirit. You saw Christ, and became Christ. So choose carefully what you give your deepest attention to. Look long enough for love that you begin recognizing it everywhere. Look deeply enough into Christ that the face you encounter there begins quietly appearing in your own.

For what you truly see, you are already beginning to become.

### Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** Let me behold You.

> **Exhale:** Let me become like You.
