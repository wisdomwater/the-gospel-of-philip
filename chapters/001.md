# Day 1 — Inheriting the Living

> “Those who inherit the dead are themselves dead, and they inherit the dead. Those who inherit the living are themselves alive, and they inherit both the living and the dead.”
>
> — *The Gospel of Philip*

There are things we can inherit without ever truly possessing them. A family can hand down traditions, stories, beliefs, even ways of seeing the world. We can receive them carefully, preserve them faithfully, and pass them along unchanged. Yet something can survive for generations this way without ever being alive.

Consider a different kind of inheritance. A slave may long for freedom, but a child receives something more: the inheritance of the father. And then comes the unsettling distinction. Those who inherit the dead are themselves dead. Those who inherit the living must themselves be alive.

The words turn the question toward us. What exactly have we inherited?

We may have inherited words such as *God*, *Christ*, *salvation*, *resurrection*, and *eternal life*. We may know the stories and sing the songs. We may know when to stand and when to sit, what to believe and what not to believe, which questions have acceptable answers and which ones make everyone in the room nervous. These things may carry something precious, but the container is not the same thing as what it contains.

Imagine someone descending into the waters of baptism and emerging saying, “I’m a Christian,” yet the name can still be borrowed. A name can be placed upon us without its reality taking root within us. We can carry the word without yet becoming what the word describes.

There is a difference between knowing about bread and eating it. Perhaps this is what it means to inherit the living. The spiritual life cannot finally be received secondhand. Someone else can tell you that God is good, but eventually goodness must become something encountered. Someone else can tell you that Christ brings life, but eventually that life must begin stirring within you. Someone else can describe resurrection, but resurrection must somehow become more than an event we expect someday after we die. It must touch the living.

This does not require us to throw away what we have inherited. The saying offers something subtler. Those who inherit the living inherit “both the living and the dead.” Once life is present, even the old forms can be received differently. Scripture, sacrament, doctrine, prayer, tradition—things that may once have seemed like artifacts from another age can become transparent to something deeper. The vessel remains, but now there is wine inside it.

Perhaps that is why spiritual awakening sometimes feels less like discovering something new and more like discovering that something familiar has suddenly become alive. A prayer you have spoken a thousand times catches in your throat. A familiar sentence of Scripture opens into a depth you had never noticed. Silence stops feeling empty. God ceases, for a moment, to be an idea you are trying to believe in and becomes Presence.

Nothing outward may have changed. But you have. Names and realities. Water and fire. Bread and wine. Anointing and resurrection. Bride and bridegroom. Something visible opens toward something invisible, as though the surface of ordinary things has become thin enough for another reality to shine through.

We do not need to approach these words as another object to collect. We don't need to master their vocabulary or force every mysterious sentence into a system. Perhaps we can simply come with open hands and ask whether something living remains here—something capable of meeting the living part of us.

For life recognizes life. The inheritance of the Father is not merely something waiting beyond death. It is already being offered. The question is whether we are becoming alive enough to receive it.

## Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** You are living.

> **Exhale:** Make me alive.
