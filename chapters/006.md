# Day 6 — Resurrection Before Death

> “Those who say the Lord first died and then arose are wrong, because he arose first and then died. If someone doesn't first receive the resurrection, they won't die, because as God lives, they would [...]”
>
> — *The Gospel of Philip*

We usually know where resurrection belongs in the story. First comes life, then death, and finally resurrection. The sequence seems so obvious that reversing it sounds almost nonsensical, yet here the order is turned upside down: Christ arose first and then died. Perhaps resurrection is larger than we thought.

If resurrection means only what happens to a body after death, then we must spend our lives waiting for it. It belongs to another world, another age, another side of the grave. We may believe in it, hope for it, and confess it, but we cannot yet experience it because the necessary first step has not happened: we have not died.

But what if resurrection begins before the grave? There are ways of being dead while our hearts are still beating. Fear can close us inward until life becomes something to defend rather than receive. Shame can convince us to hide the parts of ourselves we believe cannot be loved, while disappointment can slowly teach us not to hope for very much at all. We continue moving through the world, doing what is expected of us, while something inside has quietly gone to sleep. Resurrection speaks to this kind of death too.

Consider the moments when something you thought was gone began to live again. Perhaps you trusted after betrayal, loved after loss, or discovered hope after becoming convinced that hope was foolish. Perhaps you found yourself laughing during a season of grief and were surprised that joy had somehow found its way back into the room.

These are small resurrections, but they teach the body the shape of rising. To receive resurrection before death is to discover a life that circumstances did not create and therefore cannot completely take away. It is not denial of suffering, and it does not require us to pretend that death is harmless. Resurrection takes death seriously enough to pass directly through it, but refuses to grant death the final word.

This changes what it means to follow Christ. We are not simply following someone who once died and later returned to life. We are following the Living One, and the invitation is to participate in that life now. Resurrection becomes more than something we believe happened to Jesus; it becomes a pattern that begins taking shape within us. Something dies, and something more alive emerges.

The self built entirely around approval may begin to loosen its grip, and underneath it we discover a person who no longer needs everyone to understand. The resentment we have carried for years may finally begin to soften, leaving room for a freedom we did not know was possible. The certainty we once needed may fall apart, only for wonder to appear where certainty used to stand.

This kind of resurrection can feel frightening because we often become attached even to the things that keep us entombed. The familiar prison may feel safer than an unfamiliar freedom. We know who we are inside the old story, even when that story hurts, while resurrection asks us to step into a life we have not yet learned how to live.

The stone moves, and suddenly there is open sky. Perhaps this is why resurrection must come first. We need to encounter a life greater than death before we can release our grip on the things we are afraid to lose. If resurrection is already becoming real within us, then letting go no longer feels quite so much like disappearance; something deeper has begun to teach us that surrender and annihilation are not the same thing.

The seed offers an image of this mystery. A seed does not wait until it is safely above the soil before surrendering its old form. It disappears into darkness, breaks open, and becomes something it could never have become while remaining intact. Yet hidden within that surrender is already the life that will eventually push through the earth toward the sun. Perhaps resurrection is already hidden within us in the same way.

There is something in us that belongs to life more deeply than it belongs to death. We may glimpse it whenever love proves stronger than resentment, whenever compassion interrupts judgment, whenever truth frees us from a story that once imprisoned us. We may feel it whenever we stop merely protecting our existence and begin offering ourselves to life.

Then even our understanding of physical death begins to shift. We do not have to pretend we know exactly what waits beyond it, nor do we need to silence the fear that naturally rises before the unknown. We can simply notice that resurrection has already begun teaching us a language that death does not understand.

We have practiced rising. Perhaps that is the invitation hidden inside this strange reversal. Do not spend your whole life waiting for resurrection to arrive someday. Receive resurrection while you are alive, and let the life of Christ begin loosening everything in you that has mistaken the tomb for home.

The grave does not get to introduce us to eternal life. We can begin learning it now.

## Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** Life is already rising.

> **Exhale:** Let me rise with You.
