# Day 24 — Anointed

> “The chrism is superior to baptism, because we're called ‘Christians’ because of the chrism, not because of the baptism. Christ was named because of the chrism, because the Father anointed the Son, the Son anointed the apostles, and the apostles anointed us.”
>
> — *The Gospel of Philip*

Oil does not remain on the surface for long. Pour it onto dry skin and it begins to soak in. What first glistens visibly slowly disappears beneath the surface, leaving the skin changed by something it has absorbed. Anointing is a fitting image for a spiritual life that is meant to penetrate rather than merely decorate us.

To be anointed is to receive something. The movement begins with the Father and flows outward: the Father anoints the Son, the Son anoints the apostles, and the anointing continues onward. No one creates the oil for themselves. What is received becomes something that can be given.

This is the same rhythm we have encountered before. Faith receives and love gives. Bread is received and becomes part of the body, while light is received and becomes something we wear. Again and again, divine life moves toward us so that it can eventually move through us.

Chrism gives this movement a name. The word *Christ* itself means the anointed one. To be called Christian, then, is to bear a name rooted in anointing. The name does not merely tell us whose ideas we believe; it points toward the life with which we are being permeated.

This returns us to the gift of the name. A borrowed name can remain on the surface. We can carry it through habit, family, culture, doctrine, or community without allowing it to travel very far inward. But anointing asks the name to soak deeper.

The oil disappears into the person. Perhaps this is why spiritual transformation can be difficult to measure. We naturally look for visible evidence, dramatic experiences, clear milestones, and unmistakable moments of change. Yet much of what matters most may be happening beneath the surface.

Oil works quietly. Patience may be entering places once governed by urgency. Compassion may be reaching a wound that once produced only defensiveness, while forgiveness may be loosening a resentment we assumed would always remain. We may not notice the transformation until one day we respond differently to something that once would have overwhelmed us. The anointing has gone deeper.

There is tenderness in this image. Transformation does not always need to happen through force, struggle, or self-conquest. Sometimes we are changed because something gentle remains in contact with us long enough to be absorbed.

Love can work this way. We sit in silence and apparently nothing happens. We return to prayer and encounter no extraordinary revelation, yet we remain. We receive the bread, breathe the prayer, practice forgiveness, notice beauty, and allow ourselves again and again to be held in the possibility that God is kind.

Slowly, the fibers take the color. The oil reaches places our arguments could not. Perhaps this is why anointing belongs with resurrection. Resurrection is not simply a future event attached to the end of life; it is divine life entering what has become dead. The anointing carries that life into us now.

Something begins to rise. The parts of us hardened by disappointment can soften. The places buried beneath shame can breathe again, and the identities built around failure can begin releasing their claim upon us. Resurrection moves through the person like oil entering dry skin.

Anointing also carries light. Oil once fed lamps, allowing flame to remain alive through the darkness. The image gathers together what we have already encountered: oil carries fire, fire gives light, and light reveals what darkness concealed. The anointed life becomes a life capable of carrying flame.

But the flame is not merely for us. A lamp does not illuminate itself alone. Its light spills outward onto whatever happens to be nearby. The transformation hidden within one person eventually changes the space around them.

This may be the quiet vocation of the anointed. We do not need to become impressive. We do not need to convince everyone that we possess special spiritual knowledge or demonstrate that we have reached some higher state. We can simply become people through whom a little more light enters the room.

Perhaps someone feels less judged in our presence. Perhaps another person discovers that they can tell the truth without being rejected. Maybe our patience creates enough space for someone else's anger to soften, or our willingness to forgive interrupts a cycle that otherwise would have continued into another generation.

The oil becomes light. This is also where the cross belongs. Love that enters the world will eventually encounter suffering because love refuses to remain safely detached. To carry the life of Christ is to become vulnerable to the pain of others rather than protecting ourselves through indifference.

Yet the cross does not stand alone. Resurrection is already within the anointing. The love that becomes vulnerable is also the life that cannot finally be extinguished by what it suffers. Cross and resurrection become movements within the same mystery of self-giving love.

Perhaps anointing means allowing that mystery to enter us. We do not merely admire Christ from a distance. We do not simply imitate behaviors while remaining untouched within. We allow the same life to soak into us until something of Christ begins appearing through our own particular humanity.

The oil disappears. The person remains. But the person carries a fragrance that was not there before.

Maybe this is what it means to bear the name Christian. Not to display a label, defend an identity, or prove membership in the correct group, but to become permeated by the life whose name we carry. The name becomes real as the anointing moves from the surface toward the heart.

The Father anoints the Son. The Son shares the anointing. And the oil continues flowing.

## Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** Anoint me with Your life.

> **Exhale:** Let Your light shine through me.
