## Day 23 — The Living Food

> “Before Christ came, there was no bread in the world. Just as Paradise, where Adam was, had many trees for animal food but no wheat for human food, people fed like animals. But when Christ, the perfect human, came, he brought bread from heaven so people could be nourished with human food.”
>
> — *The Gospel of Philip*

We are always eating. The body takes in food, breaks it apart, and quietly turns what was outside us into what is inside us. Bread becomes blood and muscle, energy and movement. What we receive eventually becomes part of the life we carry into the world.

The same may be true of things we never place in our mouths. We consume stories, images, conversations, arguments, fears, desires, and opinions. We take in the moods of rooms and the anxieties of communities, often without noticing how deeply they enter us. Much of this happens so automatically that we rarely ask what kind of person our diet is creating.

Some food keeps us alive without teaching us how to live. There is food for survival and food for becoming human. We can fill ourselves endlessly and remain strangely hungry. We can consume more information, entertainment, recognition, possessions, achievement, and certainty while the deeper hunger remains untouched.

The appetite simply asks for more. This is one of the strange qualities of consuming what cannot nourish us. Satisfaction arrives briefly and then disappears, leaving us reaching again for another portion. The cycle can continue long after we have forgotten what hunger we were originally trying to satisfy.

Perhaps the hunger itself deserves our attention. There is a longing beneath many of our appetites that is not easily named. We want to know that we matter, that we belong, that our lives have meaning, and that we are held within something larger than ourselves. We want to be loved without constantly proving that we deserve love.

These are human hungers. They cannot always be satisfied by the things nearest at hand. Approval may taste like belonging for a moment, while achievement may briefly resemble worth. Pleasure can imitate peace, and certainty can feel almost like trust.

But eventually the hunger returns. Bread from heaven offers another kind of nourishment. It does not merely satisfy an appetite; it nourishes the human being we are becoming. Compassion feeds something different within us than contempt does, just as gratitude forms us differently than envy. What we consume shapes our capacity to live.

Consider what happens when we feed repeatedly on outrage. The world begins appearing primarily as a collection of offenses, enemies, threats, and fools. Even when the outrage is justified, a steady diet of it can train the heart to search constantly for the next thing to despise. The food becomes the eater.

The same is true of fear. If we continually consume messages telling us that everything is collapsing and everyone outside our circle is dangerous, suspicion begins feeling like wisdom. We may call it discernment while slowly becoming unable to encounter another person without first deciding which side they belong to.

What we eat becomes our way of seeing. But there is living food. We can feed on mercy until mercy becomes easier to recognize. We can receive beauty until wonder begins returning to ordinary things, and we can practice gratitude until abundance becomes visible in places scarcity once dominated our attention.

This is not denial. Living food does not require us to pretend suffering is unreal or injustice does not matter. Compassion can look directly at pain without feeding on hatred. Hope can acknowledge darkness without allowing darkness to become the only thing we know how to see.

Christ offers human food. The life we encounter there does not make us less human but more deeply human. We see someone who can grieve without surrendering to despair, become angry without becoming cruelty, and suffer without allowing suffering to extinguish love. Divine life appears not as an escape from humanity but as humanity brought toward fullness.

Perhaps this is what we are meant to consume. Bread is not admired from across the room. We do not study its ingredients forever or construct elaborate theories about its meaning while leaving it untouched on the table. Bread fulfills its purpose by being received.

Then something mysterious happens. The bread disappears. What was outside us becomes part of us. We can no longer point to it as something separate because it has entered the body and become life. Nourishment has completed its work when the distinction between the food and the one being fed begins to disappear.

This is a powerful image of communion. We receive Christ not merely to possess Christ but to become shaped by the life we receive. Mercy becomes our mercy, compassion becomes our compassion, and the love of the Father begins finding expression through our particular hands and voice. The living food becomes a living person.

This makes attention to our diet a spiritual practice. We can notice what we repeatedly place before our eyes and minds, what conversations we return to, and what stories about ourselves we rehearse. We can ask a simple question without turning it into another reason for shame: *What is this feeding in me?*

Some things feed fear. Some feed resentment, comparison, numbness, or the endless need for more. Other things feed patience, courage, tenderness, curiosity, gratitude, and the capacity to remain present.

We do not need to judge ourselves for being hungry. Hunger is part of being alive. The question is whether we are learning to recognize the food that can actually meet it. Beneath all our smaller appetites may be a deeper longing for the life from which we came.

The bread is already on the table. Receive what gives life. Let it enter deeply enough that it becomes difficult to tell where the gift ends and your own life begins. Then become nourishment yourself, offering to the world what has been given to you.

### Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** Feed me with Your life.

> **Exhale:** Let me become living bread.
