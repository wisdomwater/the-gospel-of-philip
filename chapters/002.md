## Day 2 — Beyond the Names

> “The names that are given to those who are worldly are very deceptive, because they turn the heart away from what's right to what's not right, and someone who hears ‘God’ doesn't think of what's right but thinks of what's not right. So also with ‘the Father,’ ‘the Son,’ ‘the Holy Spirit,’ ‘the life,’ ‘the light,’ ‘the resurrection,’ ‘the church,’ and all the others.”
>
> — *The Gospel of Philip*

Say the word *God*. Something happens almost immediately. An image forms. Perhaps you imagine an old man somewhere above the clouds, or a king seated upon a throne. Perhaps you remember the God you were taught about as a child, the God of a particular church or creed, the God invoked in moments of gratitude and blamed in moments of suffering. The word is only three letters long, yet an entire world can hide inside it.

And that is the danger of names. We need them. Without names, it would be nearly impossible to speak to one another about the deepest things. We say *Father*, *Son*, *Spirit*, *life*, *light*, *resurrection*. The names become vessels in which we carry realities too large to hold in our hands. Yet eventually we can forget that they are vessels. We begin to mistake the word for the thing itself.

Consider the word *Father*. For one person it may carry tenderness, protection, warmth, and belonging. For another it may carry distance, disappointment, or fear. Both people can hear exactly the same word while encountering entirely different realities. When we bring that word into prayer, everything we have known about fathers may quietly come with it. And then we may assume that what we have imagined is God.

The same thing happens with *church*. Perhaps the word awakens memories of music, candles, communion, friendship, and belonging. Perhaps it awakens memories of judgment, exclusion, pressure, or shame. Perhaps it carries both. A single word becomes crowded with years of experience until it becomes difficult to distinguish the thing itself from everything that has accumulated around its name.

This is why names can deceive us. Not necessarily because the names are false, but because they can convince us that we already know what they mean.

Yet there is tenderness hidden inside this problem. Truth gives us names because we need them. How else could we begin? A child learns the word *light* before understanding photons or stars. We learn the word *love* long before we understand how much joy and grief that little word will eventually contain. Names give us somewhere to begin. But they are beginnings.

There is a name that cannot be spoken in the world, a name the Father gives to the Son. Those who possess it know it, yet do not say it. Perhaps some realities become smaller the moment we try to capture them in language. There are things known not because we can define them, but because we have encountered them.

Anyone who has loved deeply already understands this. You can say someone's name, describe their appearance, recount their history, and tell stories about them for hours. Yet none of those things is the person. The person always exceeds the description. Love knows something that language can approach but never contain.

How much more must this be true of God? Perhaps spiritual maturity involves allowing our words to become spacious again. *Father* can become larger than our history with fathers. *Christ* can become larger than our arguments about Christ. *Church* can become larger than the institutions that have carried its name. *Resurrection* can become larger than the picture we have formed of something that happens after death.

Even *God* can become mysterious again. This does not mean abandoning language. It means holding language with humility. We can speak the names while remembering that they point beyond themselves. We can say *light* while allowing ourselves to be surprised by illumination. We can say *life* while admitting that we may not yet know what it means to be fully alive. We can say *Father* while leaving room for the One behind the name to reveal what fatherhood actually means.

“There’s only one Truth, but it’s many things for us.” Perhaps this is mercy. The One comes to us through many things because one image could never contain the whole. Bread speaks. Water speaks. Light speaks. A father speaks. A mother speaks. A bride and bridegroom speak. Death and resurrection speak. Each image reveals something, and each eventually reaches the edge of what it can say.

Beyond that edge is mystery. Prayer may sometimes require allowing ourselves to reach that edge. We speak the names we have been given, and then we become quiet. We loosen our grip on the definitions. We allow God to be greater than the God we have imagined.

The name has brought us to the doorway. We do not have to remain at the doorway.

### Breath Prayer

> **Inhale:** Beyond every name,

> **Exhale:** let me know You.
