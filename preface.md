## Preface

Some ancient books seem familiar almost immediately. Their stories move through landscapes we recognize, their characters have become part of our shared imagination, and even before we open them we have some idea of where they are going. The *Gospel of Philip* is not one of those books.

It does not begin with the birth of Jesus, gather disciples along the Sea of Galilee, or lead us toward Jerusalem and an empty tomb. There is no continuous story at all. Instead, we encounter sayings, fragments, theological reflections, interpretations of scripture, and strange, luminous images that appear for a moment before giving way to something else.

Bread. Water. Oil. Fire. Light. A pearl in the mud, a name that cannot be spoken, a bride and bridegroom entering a hidden chamber. Philip gives us images and seems content to let us live inside them for a while.

The text we know as the *Gospel of Philip* survived in a Coptic manuscript discovered with the Nag Hammadi library in Egypt in 1945. It is preserved in the same codex that contains the *Gospel of Thomas*, although the two works are quite different in character. The surviving manuscript is centuries removed from the beginnings of Christianity, while the work itself is generally placed within the rich theological world of early Christianity and is commonly associated with Valentinian thought.

That world was still wrestling with questions that later generations would answer with creeds, councils, and carefully developed systems of theology. Christians were asking who Christ was, what salvation meant, what resurrection promised, and how the divine could be encountered within material human life. Baptism, Eucharist, anointing, resurrection, incarnation, and the relationship between Father and Son were not merely settled doctrines to be repeated; they were mysteries being explored from many directions.

Philip comes to us from within that conversation.

Calling it a *gospel* can therefore create expectations the book has no intention of satisfying. It is not another biography of Jesus or an alternative account of his ministry. It feels at times more like entering a room in which a teacher has left scraps of conversation, reflections, interpretations, and symbols scattered across a table.

Some of those fragments fit together easily. Others seem to contradict one another or assume a symbolic world that is no longer ours. Still others contain a startling beauty that can cross seventeen centuries with almost no assistance.

One of the best ways to approach Philip may be to resist the urge to solve it too quickly.

Modern readers are accustomed to extracting meaning. We want to know what a passage means, what conclusion we should draw from it, and how it fits into a coherent theological system. Philip often refuses to cooperate with that desire.

Instead, it gives us an image.

Truth does not enter the world naked but comes clothed in images. Water contains another water, while oil carries hidden fire. Those who see Christ become Christ, and those who receive light put on the light until the powers that once held them can no longer possess them.

These images do not behave like definitions. They open rather than close. They invite us to remain with something long enough for its meaning to become larger rather than smaller.

That became the approach of this book.

The thirty readings that follow are not intended as a new translation of the *Gospel of Philip*, nor are they a scholarly commentary on Valentinian Christianity. Each begins instead with a passage from Philip and allows its imagery to become a doorway. Other themes and images from the gospel sometimes enter the meditation, not to construct a definitive interpretation but to allow Philip's symbolic world to speak in a contemplative modern language.

A recurring theme soon emerged: **participation**.

Philip seems deeply interested in the difference between knowing something and becoming something. Faith receives, love gives, and truth is not merely understood but entered. Resurrection is not only something awaited after death; it is something to be received while we are alive.

The same movement appears again and again. Bread is eaten, oil soaks into the body, light is put on, and the name Christian is received rather than merely borrowed. To see Christ is somehow to become Christ, as though contemplation reaches its fulfillment not when we have correctly described what we behold but when something of it has begun appearing within us.

This is a spirituality that continually moves from surface toward depth.

The visible carries the hidden. Water can contain water, oil can contain fire, and the ordinary world can become transparent to something more than itself. Even the human being becomes mysterious: there is a human within the human, an interior world that cannot be reduced to what is visible from outside.

This way of seeing does not require us to despise the material world. Quite the opposite, the material world becomes the place through which mystery arrives. Bread, bodies, water, touch, marriage, hunger, light, and oil become garments through which invisible realities make themselves known.

Philip's language can nevertheless be strange, and sometimes it should remain strange.

Its bridal chamber imagery can feel foreign to modern Christian ears. Its sacramental language does not always fit comfortably within later categories, and some of its language about gender, cosmology, resurrection, and spiritual powers belongs unmistakably to another time and theological world. This devotional does not attempt to erase that distance or pretend that Philip was secretly a twenty-first-century contemplative writer.

Instead, I have tried to let the distance remain while asking what might still cross it.

Sometimes an ancient image survives because human beings have not changed as much as our surroundings have. We still know what it is to feel divided within ourselves, to hunger for something we cannot name, and to discover that shame becomes stronger when hidden. We still know the difference between being told that we are loved and somehow learning to receive that love as true.

We still long for union.

That longing may be the thread running beneath many of Philip's strangest images. Separation gives way to communion, the hidden becomes visible, the divided becomes whole, and the one standing before the light eventually discovers that the light is something that can be worn. Salvation begins to look less like escaping somewhere else and more like participating deeply in divine life.

Throughout these readings, I have approached that divine life through the conviction that the Father revealed in Christ is not a tyrant from whom Christ must rescue us. The mercy we encounter in Christ does not need to be wrestled from an unwilling God. If Christ makes the Father known, then the compassion, tenderness, forgiveness, and self-giving love we encounter in Christ reveal something about the Source from whom Christ comes.

This is a contemplative lens, and I do not offer it as the only possible interpretation of Philip's theological world. But it has shaped the way I have listened to these fragments. Again and again, I have heard beneath them an invitation away from fear and toward participation, away from separation and toward communion, away from merely possessing religious language and toward becoming alive.

That movement also explains the breath prayers at the end of each reading.

A breath prayer is intentionally small. After several pages of words, it reduces the meditation to two short phrases carried by the body's simplest rhythm. We breathe in, we breathe out, and for a moment we stop trying to understand everything.

Perhaps that is an appropriate way to read Philip.

There will be passages you understand immediately and others that remain opaque. Some images may become meaningful only after you have carried them for a while, while others may never become yours at all. There is no need to force them.

Read slowly enough to notice what opens.

Allow the pearl to remain in your hand for a while. Sit beside the water, feel the oil on the skin, watch the fire, taste the bread, and enter the bridal chamber without demanding that every symbol immediately surrender its meaning. Let the images do what images have always done: carry us toward realities that words alone cannot contain.

Thirty days from now, Philip may still be strange.

I hope it is.

But perhaps the strangeness will have become spacious rather than threatening. Perhaps somewhere along the way an ancient image will become transparent, and through it you will glimpse something that was already quietly present within your own life.

Truth comes clothed in images.

For the next thirty days, we will sit with them and see what they reveal.
